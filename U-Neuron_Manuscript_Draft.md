## Abstract

We introduce the U-Neuron, a novel neural network layer whose computational substrate is the U-Space number system, a structured algebraic extension of the real line defined as the set **Z** = {z = x + e**i** | x ∈ ℝ, e ≥ ε₀}, where x carries classical activation energy, e encodes an infinitesimal fiber magnitude that functions as a curvature regime selector, and **i** satisfies **i**² = −1. The U-Neuron is distinguished by four architectural properties that operate in concert: a mandatory cross-coupling forward transformation that prevents algebraic degeneration into parallel real-valued transforms; an Emission Boundary defined by emit(**z**) = √(x² + e²) that acts as an irreversible projection from the complex representational substrate to a classical scalar observable; a Landauer Regularizer of the form **L** = λ·β·mean(√((δx)² + (δe)² + ε)) that embeds per-layer thermodynamic cost accounting as a first-class architectural primitive rather than a global loss supplement; and three interchangeable Constraint Manifold Variants — General (ℝⁿˣᵐ), Doubly Stochastic (Birkhoff polytope Bₙ), and Unitary (U(n)) — all sharing the same underlying U-Space algebra and emission mechanism. Two activation functions are defined within this algebra: CReLU, which applies standard nonlinearities to the real component while bounding the fiber component through a softplus gate; and modReLU, which implements a self-gating mechanism via the composite magnitude r = √(x² + e²), producing emergent uncertainty signals through the e channel without explicit supervision. Preliminary empirical evidence supports the architecture's utility: training with Landauer coefficient λ = 0.1 improves MNIST classification accuracy from 0.984 to 0.987; the e channel exhibits statistically significant correlation with quantum tomography error (Pearson r = +0.336); and the modReLU variant achieves out-of-distribution detection at AUROC 0.556 with zero OOD-specific supervision. We situate U-Neuron within and against six contemporary research lineages — complex-valued neural networks, unitary recurrent architectures, state space models, DeepSeek's manifold-constrained hyper-connections, p-adic neural networks, and the emerging field of thermodynamic machine learning — and identify four axes of genuine architectural novelty. The architecture is assessed as version 0.1.0: research-grade, empirically promising, and theoretically substantive, but requiring scaled validation to establish definitive claims.

## 1. Introduction: The Inadequacy of ℝⁿ and the Promise of Structured Fiber Extensions

The prevailing computational paradigm for artificial neural networks is grounded in the Euclidean real vector space ℝⁿ, a choice that is historically accidental as much as it is principled. Real-valued architectures achieve universal function approximation under mild conditions [3], and their gradient dynamics are well characterized by a mature body of optimization theory. Yet the very tractability of ℝⁿ comes at a cost: the space possesses no native mechanism for encoding curvature, no intrinsic representation of uncertainty, and no algebraic structure that distinguishes reversible from irreversible computation. When the phenomena a network must model are themselves curved, uncertain, or irreversible — as in quantum state estimation, physical simulation, anomaly detection, or hierarchical reasoning — the practitioner must graft these properties onto a fundamentally flat substrate through engineering heuristics: Bayesian wrappers, ensemble methods, dropout at inference time, auxiliary loss terms, and external uncertainty heads. The U-Neuron proposes a different approach: to redesign the representational substrate so that these properties arise naturally from the algebra itself.

The observation that ℝⁿ is insufficient as a universal computational medium is not new. A substantial body of contemporary research has explored extensions to richer algebraic structures, each with distinct topological and geometric consequences. Complex-valued neural networks (CVNNs) operate over ℂ, exploiting the Wirtinger calculus and the phase-magnitude decomposition of complex numbers to process wave-like signals, electromagnetic fields, and quantum amplitudes with superior parameter efficiency [1, 2, 3]. Hypercomplex architectures extend further into quaternion and octonion algebras, achieving parameter reductions by factors of four and eight respectively while natively encoding the geometric product structure of three-dimensional rotations [6]. Clifford and geometric algebra networks generalize still further, defining multivector representations that support grade-structured geometric products of the form **uv** = **u**·**v** + **u**∧**v**, where the scalar inner product and the bivector wedge product coexist within a single computational object [Deep Research 1]. And non-Archimedean architectures operating over the p-adic numbers Q_p abandon Euclidean geometry entirely, replacing the standard absolute value with an ultrametric that satisfies the strong triangle inequality d(x,y) ≤ max(d(x,z), d(z,y)) and thereby generates hierarchically nested balls that model tree-structured and fractal data with exactness that Euclidean approximation cannot achieve [17, 18, 19]. Each of these extensions incurs hardware and software costs — quaternion and p-adic arithmetic have no native GPU support, and full hypercomplex gradient computation requires either specialized implementations or significant computational overhead [Deep Research 1].

U-Neuron occupies a distinct position within this landscape. Rather than extending ℝⁿ into a higher-dimensional or non-Archimedean field, it equips each real activation with a constrained positive fiber — a second real coordinate e bounded below by a small positive constant ε₀ ≈ 10⁻⁸ — and treats the pair (x, e) as a single complex-valued entity z = x + e**i** under the standard imaginary unit. This construction is strictly implementable in existing real-valued frameworks with only modestly increased parameter count, since the U-Space algebra reduces to two coupled real-valued linear maps rather than a new numeric type. The fiber component e functions not merely as a second activation channel but as a curvature regime selector: its magnitude selects among different geometric regimes of the foliated structure defined by the U-Space metric, and its learned dynamics under the Landauer regularizer generate an emergent thermodynamic cost signal that penalizes informationally expensive state transitions. The Emission Boundary then collapses this two-dimensional representation into a single classical scalar in a manner that is deliberately and architecturally irreversible, creating a structural analog of quantum measurement within the network's forward pass. These properties — infinitesimal fiber geometry, per-layer thermodynamic accounting, and controlled irreversibility — constitute the manuscript's central claims, and each is developed at length in what follows.

This paper makes four principal contributions. First, it provides a rigorous mathematical specification of the U-Space algebra and all derived architectural components, including the forward transformation, emission operator, Landauer regularizer, and activation functions. Second, it situates U-Neuron with precision against six academic research lineages, identifying what is inherited, what is adapted, and what is genuinely new. Third, it develops a theoretical account of U-Neuron's behavior through three intersecting frameworks: constraint manifold geometry, information thermodynamics, and the quantum measurement analogy for neural network inference. Fourth, it presents a frank novelty assessment against the current literature, acknowledging where the Landauer-ML intersection is already active while arguing for the specificity and significance of U-Neuron's implementation. The manuscript proceeds through the architecture's mathematical foundations (Section 2), a survey of the relevant academic landscape (Section 3), a deep treatment of constraint manifold geometry (Section 4), the thermodynamic grounding of the architecture (Section 5), the quantum measurement analogy and its formal consequences (Section 6), the novelty assessment (Section 7), and open theoretical questions (Section 8), before concluding in Section 9.

## 2. Mathematical Foundations of the U-Space Architecture

### 2.1 The U-Space Algebraic System: Z = {z = x + e**i** | x ∈ ℝ, e ≥ ε₀}

The U-Space number system is defined as the set **Z** = {z = x + e**i** | x ∈ ℝ, e ∈ ℝ, e ≥ ε₀}, where x is the classical activation component, e is the infinitesimal fiber magnitude, **i** is the standard imaginary unit satisfying **i**² = −1, and ε₀ is a small positive floor constant fixed at 10⁻⁸ in the reference implementation. This construction differs from the full complex field ℂ in one structurally significant respect: the imaginary component e is constrained to be strictly positive, bounded below by ε₀. The consequence of this constraint is that U-Space does not admit zero fiber values, which would correspond to a degenerate projection back onto the real line ℝ. Every element of **Z** therefore carries a nonzero curvature signature, and the magnitude √(x² + e²) is always strictly positive — a property that ensures the Emission Boundary remains well-defined and nonsingular throughout training. The space **Z** can be given the product topology of ℝ × ℝ_{>0}, which is homeomorphic to a half-open strip and is algebraically distinct from both the standard complex field and the p-adic numbers Q_p: the latter are equipped with an ultrametric topology deriving from the p-adic absolute value |·|_p, which generates the characteristic strong triangle inequality and gives rise to totally disconnected, hierarchically nested ball structures [17, 18, 19]. U-Space, by contrast, inherits its topology from the Euclidean metric on its two real components, and the Riemannian norm ‖z‖ = √(x² + e²) defines a genuine Riemannian metric on the half-strip ℝ × ℝ_{>0}.

The fiber component e admits a geometric interpretation through foliation theory. The family of sets {z ∈ **Z** : e = c} for varying positive constants c defines a foliation of U-Space into horizontal leaves, each isomorphic to ℝ and carrying a distinct curvature signature determined by the leaf index c. As e varies continuously with depth during the forward pass, the network's representation traces a path through different curvature regimes of this foliated structure. This is conceptually analogous to the role of the imaginary component in dual numbers (where ε² = 0, restricting information to first-order neighborhood) or the phase component in complex numbers (where e can be negative, allowing destructive interference), but it is algebraically distinct from both: U-Space enforces strict positivity of e, preventing degeneration to the real line, while retaining the complex product structure that enables the cross-coupling terms described in Section 2.2. The connection to nonstandard analysis and hyperreal numbers is suggestive rather than rigorous at this stage: e is conceptualized as an infinitesimal in the sense that it is a very small positive quantity, but U-Space as currently defined is a standard mathematical object with no formal connection to Robinson's nonstandard extension of ℝ [32, 33]. Formalizing this connection is identified as an open problem in Section 8.5.

### 2.2 The Forward Operation and the Cross-Coupling Obligation

The fundamental U-Neuron computation is the affine transformation z' = w·z + b, where the weight w, the input z, and the bias b are all elements of U-Space. The weight matrix in the reference implementation is parameterized as a complex-valued matrix W = W_a + W_b**i** ∈ ℂⁿˣᵐ, and the forward pass expands by the distributive law of complex multiplication into two coupled real-valued operations:

Re(z') = W_a · x − W_b · e + b_x

Im(z') = W_a · e + W_b · x + b_e

where x and e are the real and fiber vectors of the input, b_x and b_e are the real and fiber components of the bias vector, and the product · denotes standard real matrix-vector multiplication. These equations are the essential algebraic engine of U-Neuron, and the cross-coupling terms −W_b · e and W_b · x deserve careful attention. The term W_b · x in the fiber update means that the imaginary (fiber) part of the output is informed by the real part of the input: the classical activation energy of the preceding layer drives the curvature regime of the current layer's representation. Conversely, −W_b · e in the real update means that the curvature signature of the input exerts influence on the classical activation of the output. Without these cross-coupling terms — that is, if W_b were constrained to zero — the two components would evolve independently, and the network would reduce to the Cartesian product of two independent real-valued transformations with no algebraic coupling between them. The cross-coupling is therefore not a design choice but an algebraic necessity: it is the mechanism by which the U-Space product structure distinguishes a U-Neuron layer from a trivially doubled real network, and it is the source of the geometric expressiveness that the fiber component is intended to provide.

This structure bears a family resemblance to the split-activation design of complex-valued neural networks, in which a complex weight W = W_a + W_b**i** is applied to a complex input z = x + e**i** through the same expansion [1, 2, 3]. The key distinction is that CVNNs permit e to take arbitrary values in ℝ, including zero and negative values, while U-Space enforces e ≥ ε₀ and e ∈ ℝ_{>0}. This positivity constraint has two important consequences: it prevents the fiber from carrying signed phase information in the CVNN sense, and it ensures that the modulus √(x² + e²) provides a strictly positive self-gating signal for the modReLU activation described in Section 2.6. The Wirtinger calculus that governs gradient computation in CVNNs [Deep Research 1] applies in a modified form to U-Space: the Wirtinger derivatives ∂f/∂z = ½(∂f/∂x − i·∂f/∂e) and ∂f/∂z̄ = ½(∂f/∂x + i·∂f/∂e) remain well-defined over U-Space as a subset of ℂ, and gradient flow through the cross-coupling terms is computed by standard automatic differentiation through the expanded real equations above.

### 2.3 The Emission Boundary: emit(**z**) = √(x² + e²) as Irreversible Projection

The Emission Boundary is the operation that extracts a classical real-valued output from the complex U-Space representation at the terminal layer of a U-Neuron stack. Its definition is:

emit(**z**) = √(x² + e²)

This is the standard modulus of the complex number z = x + e**i**, computed element-wise over the output vector. The name "Emission" is chosen deliberately to evoke the irreversible character of the operation: once emit(**z**) has been applied, the fiber component e is no longer separately accessible. The classical observer receives only the collapsed magnitude, not the full U-Space representation from which it was computed. In the reference implementation, this irreversibility is enforced mechanically: the depth counter tracked by the ULinear module prohibits UEmission from being called inside the forward pass of any ULinear layer. This architectural constraint prevents the emission from being inadvertently applied mid-network, where it would collapse the two-dimensional representation prematurely and forfeit the cross-coupling dynamics that constitute the architecture's computational core.

The Emission Boundary has a precise mathematical analog in quantum mechanics, and we develop this analogy in detail in Section 6. For now, it suffices to note that the operation is structurally identical to the Born rule's collapse of a wavefunction ψ = x + e**i** to the probability |ψ|² = x² + e² — save that U-Neuron emits the modulus rather than the squared modulus, which preserves the Riemannian metric structure and avoids the nonlinearity of squaring. This choice is not merely notational: the modulus √(x² + e²) defines the Riemannian distance of the point (x, e) from the origin of U-Space, and its use as the emission operator means that the output of a U-Neuron stack is a genuine Riemannian distance rather than an arbitrary nonlinear function of the two components. This property connects U-Neuron to the growing literature on Riemannian neural networks [28, 29, 30], wherein the geometric structure of the representational space is designed to align with the intrinsic geometry of the data manifold.

### 2.4 The Landauer Regularizer: **L** = λ·β·mean(√((δx)² + (δe)² + ε))

The Landauer Regularizer is the thermodynamic accounting mechanism embedded in the U-Neuron architecture. Its formulation is:

**L**_Landauer = λ · β · mean(√((δx)² + (δe)² + ε))

where δx = x_{l+1} − x_l and δe = e_{l+1} − e_l are the per-element changes in the real and fiber components between successive layers, ε = 10⁻⁸ is a numerical stabilizer, λ ∈ ℝ_{≥0} is a global regularization strength hyperparameter, and β = 1/(k_B·T) is a temperature-like inverse scaling factor derived from Boltzmann's constant k_B and an effective temperature T. The regularizer computes, for each neuron, the Euclidean distance traversed in the two-dimensional U-Space between consecutive layers, averaged over the batch and weighted by the thermodynamic inverse temperature β. Its interpretation is grounded in Landauer's Principle [21], which establishes that any logically irreversible computation — specifically, the erasure of one bit of information — must dissipate a minimum energy of E_L = k_B·T·ln(2) as heat into the environment. The Landauer Regularizer penalizes U-Space state changes under the assumption that large state changes correspond to informationally costly transitions: a representation that changes dramatically between layers is implicitly erasing and recreating more information than one that changes smoothly, and the regularizer assigns this process a thermodynamic cost proportional to β.

The theoretical motivation for this regularizer connects to a rich and rapidly expanding literature on thermodynamic machine learning, which we survey in detail in Section 5. For now, three contextualizing observations are important. First, the regularizer operates per-layer rather than as a global loss term: the distance √((δx)² + (δe)²) is computed between each consecutive pair of layers during the forward pass, and all these per-layer penalties are accumulated into the total loss. This means the architecture embeds thermodynamic accountability as a local, layer-wise constraint on information dynamics, rather than as a global constraint on the input-output mapping. This distinction is architecturally significant because it shapes the gradient field throughout the depth of the network, not merely at its surface. Second, the β parameter introduces a temperature-like scaling that permits principled annealing schedules: reducing T (increasing β) during training progressively raises the cost of large state changes, encouraging the network to find solutions in which information flows smoothly through the representation layers. This is analogous to the simulated annealing schedule studied in the thermodynamic analysis of Hopfield networks, wherein the order parameter transitions from zero to one as the fictitious temperature drops below a critical value T_c [Deep Research 3]. Third, the choice to penalize the Euclidean distance in U-Space — rather than, say, the KL divergence between successive activation distributions — is motivated by the Riemannian metric structure of U-Space: the term √((δx)² + (δe)²) is precisely the geodesic distance in U-Space between successive layer representations under the standard flat metric, making the regularizer a direct measure of Riemannian path length through the representational manifold.

Preliminary evidence supports the regularizer's utility. On the MNIST classification benchmark, increasing λ from 0 to 0.1 improves test accuracy from 0.984 to 0.987 — a modest but consistent improvement that is reminiscent of the information compression effect described by the Information Bottleneck principle, wherein models that discard task-irrelevant information achieve better generalization [23, 27]. This result suggests that the Landauer Regularizer is functioning as intended: it is not merely penalizing large weights (as L2 regularization does) but specifically penalizing informationally expensive state dynamics, which forces the network to find representations that carry task-relevant information efficiently across layers.

### 2.5 Constraint Manifold Variants: General, Doubly Stochastic, and Unitary

The weight matrix W = W_a + W_b**i** in a U-Neuron layer can be constrained to any of three manifolds, yielding three architectural variants that share the same U-Space algebra and emission mechanism while differing in their spectral and geometric properties. The General variant places no constraint on W beyond the standard initialization strategies (Kaiming or Xavier), leaving W free to range over all of ℝⁿˣᵐ (for the real and imaginary weight components separately). This variant offers maximum representational expressiveness at the cost of potentially unbounded spectral norms, and is appropriate for shallow networks or for use with strong external optimization controls. The Doubly Stochastic variant constrains W_a and W_b each to the Birkhoff polytope Bₙ — the convex polytope of n×n doubly stochastic matrices satisfying non-negativity, row-sum unity, and column-sum unity — through iterative Sinkhorn-Knopp projection during training [16]. This constraint bounds the spectral norm of the weight matrices to at most 1 and limits signal amplification to approximately 1.6× in practice, as demonstrated by DeepSeek's mHC architecture [16]. The Unitary variant constrains W to the unitary group U(n), enforcing W*W = I and thereby preserving the L₂ norm of every input vector exactly: ‖Wz‖ = ‖z‖ for all z. This variant eliminates vanishing and exploding gradients through dynamical isometry — the singular values of the input-output Jacobian concentrate exactly at 1 — and connects U-Neuron to the substantial literature on unitary and orthogonal recurrent networks [7, 8, 9].

The significance of offering three interchangeable constraint variants on a single algebraic core cannot be overstated from an architectural perspective. No prior neural network architecture provides this modularity: CVNNs operate with unconstrained complex weights, unitary RNNs are committed to the full isometry of U(n), and DeepSeek's mHC enforces doubly stochastic constraints on multi-stream mixing matrices without the underlying complex algebra. U-Neuron unifies these three design philosophies under one representation, allowing a practitioner to select the stability-expressivity trade-off appropriate for their task while retaining the thermodynamic and geometric properties of the U-Space substrate. The trade-offs among the three variants are analyzed in depth in Section 4, where the Homogeneity Trap of doubly stochastic constraints and the Orthogonal Capacity Bottleneck of unitary constraints are treated as formal theoretical concerns rather than empirical inconveniences.

### 2.6 Activation Functions in U-Space: CReLU and modReLU Self-Gating

Two activation functions are defined within the U-Space framework, each embodying a different philosophy regarding how the fiber component e should be processed. The CReLU activation applies standard nonlinear functions — ReLU, tanh, or GELU — to the real component x, while applying a softplus gate to the fiber component e with a floor constraint enforcing e ∈ [ε₀, 10.0]. This design separates the processing of the two components: the real component undergoes standard nonlinear compression while the fiber component is kept strictly positive and bounded by a smooth monotonic gate. The CReLU design is conservative in the sense that it treats x and e as semi-independent quantities, applying distinct functional forms to each while maintaining the shared Riemannian norm that will be used at emission time. It is analogous in spirit to the split-activation design common in complex-valued networks [1, 4], wherein the real and imaginary parts of a complex activation are processed by independent real-valued functions rather than a holomorphic one. The choice of softplus rather than ReLU for the fiber component is motivated by the need to maintain strict positivity: ReLU would collapse small fiber values to zero, violating the e ≥ ε₀ constraint that is definitional for U-Space.

The modReLU activation is the more architecturally distinctive of the two. It computes the composite magnitude r = √(x² + e²) and uses this magnitude to gate both components simultaneously:

z_out = ReLU(r − threshold) / (r + ε) · z_in

where the division is element-wise and z_in = x + e**i** is the input complex vector. This operation gates the entire U-Space vector by the ReLU of its modulus minus a learned or fixed threshold, normalized by the modulus itself. When r < threshold, the gate closes and both x and e are driven toward zero (subject to the ε₀ floor on e); when r > threshold, both components are passed through with a magnitude-dependent scaling. The self-gating property arises from the fact that the gating signal r is itself a function of e: units with large fiber values are more likely to remain active regardless of their real component, while units with small fiber values will gate closed even if their real component is large. This creates a form of learned uncertainty routing: the e channel effectively signals the network's confidence in the activation, and the modReLU architecture uses that confidence signal to control information flow. This mechanism is structurally analogous to the phase-magnitude (polar) activation functions studied in complex-valued networks, such as the cardioid activation and the modReLU variant of Arjovsky et al. [7], but it is implemented over the constrained positive fiber of U-Space rather than over the full complex phase, which gives the gating signal a thermodynamic interpretation: a unit with e near ε₀ is a unit in a minimal-curvature regime, carrying minimal thermodynamic cost, while a unit with large e is in a high-curvature regime whose gating behavior has correspondingly large thermodynamic implications.

The emergence of out-of-distribution detection capability from the modReLU design — demonstrated at AUROC 0.556 without any OOD-specific supervision — provides early empirical support for the claim that the e channel carries genuine uncertainty information that is accessible to downstream classifiers or detectors through the self-gating dynamics. This result is discussed further in Section 8.2.

## 3. The Academic Landscape: Six Research Lineages

### 3.1 Complex-Valued and Hypercomplex Neural Networks

The extension of neural network computation from the real field ℝ to the complex field ℂ has a history that predates the deep learning era, but has received sustained renewed attention since Trabelsi et al.'s demonstration that deep complex networks outperform their real-valued counterparts on music transcription and speech synthesis tasks [1]. The central mathematical advantage of complex-valued networks lies in their loss surface geometry: for shallow networks with quadratic activations, the optimization landscape over ℂ has been shown to contain no spurious local minima — all poor local minima in the real-valued version become escapable saddle points when the same network is lifted to the complex domain [Deep Research 1]. This result, which has no equally clean analog in the real domain, derives from the richer algebraic structure of ℂ and the corresponding richness of the gradient flow dynamics governed by Wirtinger calculus. The Wirtinger derivative ∂f/∂z = ½(∂f/∂x − i·∂f/∂e) and its conjugate ∂f/∂z̄ = ½(∂f/∂x + i·∂f/∂e) together provide a complete characterization of gradients for non-holomorphic functions — which all practically useful complex-valued activations must be, given Liouville's theorem, which proves that every bounded entire function on ℂ must be constant, precluding the existence of simultaneously non-linear, bounded, and globally differentiable complex activations [Deep Research 1]. In practice, complex-valued networks employ split activations such as CReLU (separate ReLU on real and imaginary parts) or phase-magnitude activations such as modReLU (gating by the complex modulus), the latter of which directly inspired U-Neuron's modReLU activation [1, 4].

Hypercomplex extensions into quaternion algebra (q = r + x**i** + y**j** + z**k** with **i**² = **j**² = **k**² = **ijk** = −1) and octonion algebra (8-dimensional with non-associative multiplication) offer further reductions in parameter count — by factors of 4 and 8 respectively relative to equivalent real-valued networks — and encode the geometric product structure of rotations natively, making them well-suited for multi-modal fusion tasks and 3D geometric learning [6]. Clifford and geometric algebra networks generalize these structures by defining a geometric product **uv** = **u**·**v** + **u**∧**v** over a vector space endowed with a quadratic form, producing a multivector algebra whose grades range from scalars (grade 0) through vectors (grade 1), bivectors (grade 2), and trivectors (grade 3) up to the full pseudoscalar. This graded structure natively encodes the geometry of physical laws — electromagnetic fields, fluid dynamics, and rigid body mechanics — and geometric algebra networks have achieved state-of-the-art performance on PDE solution tasks including the Navier-Stokes equations, without requiring the computationally expensive spherical harmonic decompositions used in equivariant architectures [Deep Research 1]. The fundamental limitation of all these hypercomplex approaches is hardware incompatibility: GPU arithmetic units are optimized for real (and to a lesser extent complex) arithmetic, and quaternion or geometric algebra operations must be decomposed into real tensor contractions at the cost of significant computational overhead [Deep Research 1]. U-Neuron is designed to inhabit a middle ground: it employs complex arithmetic (two real channels with cross-coupling), which is natively supported by existing frameworks through the separation of W_a and W_b matrices, while constraining the fiber component in a way that gives the imaginary channel a specific geometric interpretation absent from generic CVNNs.

### 3.2 p-Adic and Ultrametric Neural Networks

The p-adic number system Q_p, for a prime p, equips the rational numbers with a metric derived from the p-adic absolute value |·|_p, under which numbers are considered close if they share many factors of p. The resulting topology is ultrametric: the distance function satisfies the strong triangle inequality d(x,y) ≤ max(d(x,z), d(z,y)) for all triples, which implies that every triangle is isosceles and that the open balls of any two radii are either disjoint or nested. This ultrametric structure generates a totally disconnected, hierarchically organized space whose ball structure corresponds exactly to a tree or a Cantor set, making Q_p a natural representational space for data with strict hierarchical organization [17, 18, 19, 20]. The p-adic CNN evolution equation formulated by Zambrano-Luna and Zuniga-Galindo [19] takes the form ∂X(x,t)/∂t = −X(x,t) + ∫_{Z_p} A(|x−y|_p) Y(y,t) dy + ∫_{Z_p} B(|x−y|_p) U(y) dy + Z(x), where the interaction kernels A and B depend only on the ultrametric distance |x−y|_p between nodes, enforcing a strictly hierarchical interaction structure in which nodes at different levels of the hierarchy interact only through their lowest common ancestor. P-adic networks have been shown to undergo phase transitions at thermodynamic limits — bifurcating from unique equilibrium states to infinitely many strange attractor states — and they appear well-suited for modeling cognitive processes, hierarchical categorization, and detecting abrupt discontinuities in sequential data [17, 18].

U-Space is algebraically distinct from Q_p in multiple fundamental ways, and the distinction is worth stating precisely because the two architectures might otherwise be superficially conflated by their shared use of a "fiber" or "subordinate" coordinate. First, U-Space is defined over ℝ × ℝ_{>0} with the product topology of Euclidean spaces, while Q_p is a non-Archimedean field with an ultrametric topology that is incompatible with the standard Euclidean metric. Second, U-Space uses the standard imaginary unit **i** with **i**² = −1, giving its arithmetic the character of complex multiplication, while p-adic arithmetic is defined by the p-adic absolute value and has no imaginary unit in the standard sense. Third, the positive floor constraint e ≥ ε₀ in U-Space is motivated by the desire to maintain a nonzero curvature signature and a well-defined Riemannian modulus, not by any ultrametric or hierarchical organization principle. The two architectures are therefore different solutions to different problems: p-adic networks are designed for hierarchical data with tree-structured distance functions, while U-Space is designed for data in which curvature, uncertainty, and thermodynamic cost are the primary representational concerns.

### 3.3 Unitary and Orthogonal Recurrent Architectures

The problem of learning long-range dependencies in recurrent neural networks is inextricably linked to the gradient pathology problem: during backpropagation through time, the Jacobian of the state update map is applied recursively over many time steps, causing gradients to either vanish (when the spectral radius of the Jacobian is less than 1) or explode (when it exceeds 1). Arjovsky, Shah, and Bengio [7] identified the solution in 2016: constraining the recurrent weight matrix to the unitary group U(n) or the orthogonal group O(n) guarantees that the spectral norm of the recurrence Jacobian is exactly 1 at every time step, achieving what is termed dynamical isometry — a condition under which the singular values of the input-output Jacobian concentrate around 1 rather than spreading geometrically across time. Wisdom et al. [8] extended this approach with a full-capacity unitary RNN parameterization, and Helfrich et al. [9] developed the scoRNN architecture using a skew-symmetric parameterization that maps onto SO(n) via the matrix exponential. The unitary/orthogonal constraint can be enforced through several mathematical mechanisms: the Lie algebra exponential map W = exp(X) where X = −X^T is skew-symmetric; the Cayley transform W = (I − X)(I + X)^{−1}; factorizations into Householder reflections or Givens rotations; or Riemannian gradient descent on the Stiefel manifold St(n,p) = {W ∈ ℝⁿˣᵖ : W^TW = I_p} via QR or polar decomposition retractions [Deep Research 4].

U-Neuron's Unitary constraint variant draws directly from this lineage, but operates in the context of the U-Space algebra rather than a standard real-valued recurrence. Constraining W in U-Neuron to U(n) means that the complex weight matrix W = W_a + W_b**i** satisfies W*W = I, which in turn means that the real and imaginary weight components satisfy W_a^TW_a + W_b^TW_b = I and W_a^TW_b = W_b^TW_a. Under this constraint, the forward pass Re(z') = W_a·x − W_b·e + b_x and Im(z') = W_a·e + W_b·x + b_e is norm-preserving: ‖z'‖² = ‖z‖² when b = 0, since the unitary transformation acts as a rotation on U-Space. This norm preservation is precisely what prevents the Homogeneity Trap described in Section 4: a unitary weight matrix cannot drive the subdominant singular values of the representational covariance toward zero, because it is by definition an isometry. The unitary variant of U-Neuron therefore inherits the gradient stability guarantees of unitary RNNs while embedding them within the richer algebraic structure of U-Space, where the fiber component e simultaneously tracks curvature and contributes to the Landauer thermodynamic penalty.

### 3.4 State Space Models and Structured Matrices

The S4 architecture [12] and its Mamba extension [12] represent a distinct approach to long-range dependency modeling through the lens of structured linear recurrences. State space models (SSMs) parameterize the recurrence using structured matrices — specifically, diagonal plus low-rank matrices derived from a continuous-time state space formulation — that admit efficient computation through the convolution theorem and diagonalization in Fourier space. Mamba introduces selective state updates that condition the recurrence parameters on the input, bridging the fixed-structure SSM with the input-dependent dynamics of attention mechanisms [12]. The recent theoretical synthesis of Dao and Gu [13] establishes that transformers and SSMs share a common structural framework — both are instances of a general sequence model class parameterized by input-dependent linear operators — which suggests that the architectural diversity of the field may be collapsible into a smaller set of fundamental primitives. U-Neuron does not compete with SSMs for the same design niche: SSMs are sequence models optimized for long-range temporal dependencies, while U-Neuron is a per-layer architectural primitive that can be composed into feedforward or recurrent architectures regardless of the sequence modeling strategy. However, the structured matrix philosophy of SSMs — using geometric constraints on weight matrices to achieve computational efficiency and stability — is philosophically aligned with U-Neuron's constraint manifold approach, and future work might explore hybrid architectures that combine U-Space representations with SSM recurrence dynamics.

### 3.5 DeepSeek's Manifold-Constrained Hyper-Connections

The DeepSeek-AI team's manifold-constrained hyper-connections (mHC) [16] constitute the most direct architectural antecedent to U-Neuron's constraint manifold framework, and deserve detailed treatment. The hyper-connection framework generalizes the residual connection by introducing learnable mixing matrices that redistribute information across multiple "residual streams" in the depth dimension. The generalized update rule takes the form x_{l+1} = H_res x_l + H_post F(H_pre x_l), where H_res, H_pre, and H_post are mixing matrices learned jointly with the network. In the unconstrained setting, H_res can amplify signals by factors reaching 3000× — a consequence of gradient descent driving the spectral norm of the mixing matrix far beyond 1 in pursuit of representational expressiveness. At scale (27B parameters), this amplification produces NaN errors and catastrophic training instability. The mHC solution projects each mixing matrix onto the Birkhoff polytope B_n using the Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm [16], which enforces doubly stochastic constraints (non-negativity, row sums and column sums equal to 1) and bounds the spectral norm to at most 1, in practice reducing amplification to approximately 1.6×. The Birkhoff-von Neumann theorem guarantees that any doubly stochastic matrix can be expressed as a convex combination of permutation matrices, providing a principled interpretation of the constrained mixing as probabilistic routing rather than arbitrary amplification. The computational overhead of mHC over unconstrained hyper-connections is approximately 6.7%, achieved through efficient kernel fusion and mixed-precision implementation in TileLang [16].

U-Neuron's doubly stochastic variant applies the same Sinkhorn-Knopp projection to the weight matrices W_a and W_b within the U-Space algebra, inheriting mHC's stability guarantees while embedding them within a more expressive representational substrate. The key difference is that mHC applies doubly stochastic constraints to mixing matrices whose purpose is to blend multi-stream residual information, while U-Neuron applies these constraints to the per-neuron weight matrices within a single-stream complex-valued forward transformation. This means that U-Neuron's doubly stochastic variant is not designed to replace mHC-style multi-stream mixing but to provide a complementary stability mechanism at the layer level, which could in principle be composed with mHC-style mixing at the depth level. The Homogeneity Trap risk that afflicts both mHC and U-Neuron's doubly stochastic variant — analyzed in depth in the following section — underscores the importance of understanding the long-depth behavior of Sinkhorn-projected matrices before deploying them in very deep architectures.

## 4. The Geometry of Constraint: Doubly Stochastic Polytopes, Unitary Groups, and the Homogeneity Trap

The choice of which manifold to constrain a neural network's weight matrices to is not merely a stability engineering decision: it is a statement about the geometry of the representational transformations one believes the network should perform. The Birkhoff polytope Bₙ and the unitary group U(n) represent two fundamentally different answers to the question of how representational energy should be conserved across a layer, and understanding the mathematical distinctions between these two manifolds is essential for understanding why U-Neuron's three-variant architecture provides something that neither alone can offer.

The Birkhoff polytope Bₙ is the set of all n×n doubly stochastic matrices — those satisfying non-negativity M_{i,j} ≥ 0, row-sum unity Σ_j M_{i,j} = 1 for all i, and column-sum unity Σ_i M_{i,j} = 1 for all j. This set forms a compact convex polytope of dimension (n−1)² within the n²-dimensional space of all real n×n matrices. The Birkhoff-von Neumann theorem [Deep Research 4] establishes that Bₙ is the convex hull of the n! permutation matrices: any doubly stochastic M can be written as M = Σ_k α_k P_k where α_k ≥ 0, Σ α_k = 1, and each P_k is a permutation matrix. This representation gives doubly stochastic transformations an elegant probabilistic interpretation as weighted averages of permutations — "soft permutations" that redistribute mass among coordinates without amplification. The spectral norm ‖M‖₂ = max singular value of M is rigorously bounded to at most 1 for all doubly stochastic matrices, and the primary eigenvalue is always 1 with associated eigenvector proportional to the all-ones vector. Crucially, the set of doubly stochastic matrices is closed under matrix multiplication: the product of any two doubly stochastic matrices is itself doubly stochastic. This compositional closure means that a stack of n doubly stochastic layers produces a doubly stochastic composite transformation, and the stability guarantee is preserved at arbitrary depth without accumulation of spectral drift [Deep Research 4].

The Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm provides an efficient mechanism for projecting arbitrary matrices onto Bₙ: it alternates between normalizing each row by its sum and each column by its sum, provably converging geometrically to the unique doubly stochastic matrix closest in the KL-divergence sense to the initial matrix. In practice, approximately 20 iterations reduce the approximation error to below 10⁻¹³. However, the iterative nature of Sinkhorn-Knopp introduces a subtle but important imperfection: any finite number of iterations leaves a residual approximation gap δ > 0, so that the actual spectral norm of the projected matrix satisfies ‖M‖₂ = 1 + δ rather than exactly 1. Over extreme depths, this residual drift compounds: a matrix with spectral norm 1.0001 composes across 500 layers to a composite transformation with spectral norm (1.0001)^{500} ≈ 1.05, a 5% amplification that, while small, is nonzero and grows without bound as depth increases. The alternative exact parameterizations — mHC-lite and BE-HC (Basis-Enhanced Hyper-Connections) [16] — address this by directly parameterizing the mixing matrix as a learned convex combination of a fixed set of permutation matrix basis elements, guaranteeing exact doubly stochasticity by construction at every step of training, with no iterative overhead and no approximation gap [Deep Research 4].

The Homogeneity Trap is the more serious structural limitation of doubly stochastic constraints, and it has been formally characterized as a consequence of the maximum-entropy bias inherent in the Sinkhorn projection [Deep Research 4]. The entropic projection that maps an unconstrained matrix onto the Birkhoff polytope does not merely enforce the row- and column-sum constraints: it also imposes a maximum-entropy bias that drives the projected matrix toward the uniform barycenter — the matrix U_n = (1/n)·**1****1**^T in which all entries are exactly 1/n and every input coordinate is mixed with equal weight into every output coordinate. As the mixing matrix approaches this uniform limit, the subdominant singular value σ₂(M) is systematically suppressed toward zero: σ₂(M) → 0. Since the primary singular value is fixed at 1 (corresponding to the all-ones eigenvector), the entire discriminative capacity of the transformation is concentrated in a single direction — the mean — while all orthogonal directions are progressively collapsed. The transformation ceases to route information among different coordinate directions and becomes an aggressive low-pass filter, preserving only the mean activation value and discarding all heterogeneous feature structure. Over many layers, this produces what has been termed "identity stagnation" [Deep Research 4]: the network retains hundreds of layers in depth but achieves effective expressive depth of only one or two, since each layer is merely re-computing a weighted average of the same mean signal. Layer normalization cannot rescue this situation: below a critical signal-to-noise ratio threshold, rescaling merely amplifies noise rather than restoring discriminative structure. This analysis implies that the doubly stochastic constraint is genuinely dangerous in very deep architectures, and that U-Neuron's doubly stochastic variant should be deployed with awareness of this risk, particularly at depths exceeding several dozen layers.

The unitary group U(n) avoids the Homogeneity Trap entirely, because unitary transformations preserve all singular values at exactly 1 by definition. A unitary W satisfies W*W = I, which means that the squared norm of any vector is preserved: ‖Wz‖² = z*W*Wz = z*Iz = ‖z‖². This isometry property means that no singular value can be suppressed, and the full representational capacity of the n-dimensional input space is preserved across every layer. The unitary constraint is therefore superior to the doubly stochastic constraint for very deep architectures with homogeneous information flow requirements, and it underlies the theoretical success of unitary RNNs for long-range temporal memory [7, 8, 9]. However, the unitary constraint comes with its own structural limitation: strict isometry means that a unitary layer can rotate or reflect the input but cannot scale individual features independently. If the learning task requires amplifying a specific feature direction while suppressing others — which is the generic case in discriminative learning — a strictly unitary layer is mathematically incapable of performing this operation without coordinate mixing. Networks constrained to unitary weights throughout their depth must rely entirely on the nonlinear activation functions to introduce the asymmetric feature scaling that a free real-valued weight matrix would achieve directly, and this places significant burden on the activations' expressiveness. The Orthogonal Capacity Bottleneck [Deep Research 4] captures this trade-off formally: orthogonal and unitary architectures exchange training instability risk for a rigid geometric constraint that may require substantially wider layers to achieve comparable representational capacity. Additionally, enforcing the unitary constraint during training is computationally expensive: QR retractions and polar decompositions are O(n³) operations, and Lie algebra parameterizations via the matrix exponential require costly inversion of the Cayley transform or truncation of the exponential series [Deep Research 4].

U-Neuron's three-variant architecture resolves this tension not by choosing one manifold or the other but by making the manifold a modular design choice that can be adapted to the depth, width, and task requirements of each deployment. A shallow U-Neuron stack (two to four layers) on a stable task is well-served by the General (unconstrained) variant. A medium-depth stack on a task requiring probabilistic routing — attention-like redistribution of representational mass — benefits from the doubly stochastic variant, with awareness of the Homogeneity Trap risk at extreme depths. A deep stack requiring strict gradient preservation — as in long-range temporal modeling — benefits from the unitary variant, with awareness of the Orthogonal Capacity Bottleneck and the associated computational cost. And crucially, all three variants share the same U-Space algebra, emission mechanism, and Landauer regularizer, so that the thermodynamic and geometric properties of U-Space are preserved regardless of the manifold choice.

## 5. Information Thermodynamics as Architectural Principle

### 5.1 From Abstract Bounds to Differentiable Regularization: The Landauer-ML Arc

The integration of thermodynamic principles into machine learning theory has followed a remarkably consistent trajectory over the past decade, moving from abstract theoretical bounds derived from statistical mechanics toward differentiable, gradient-compatible regularization mechanisms that can be embedded directly into neural network training. The foundational principle was established by Rolf Landauer in 1961 [21]: any logically irreversible computation — specifically, any operation that maps multiple distinct input states to a single output state — must dissipate a minimum energy of E_L = k_B·T·ln(2) per erased bit as heat into the environment. This is not a practical limitation but a fundamental physical law: it applies to any physical realization of logically irreversible computation, whether in silicon transistors, biological neurons, or photonic switching elements. Landauer's Principle was generalized to continuous-variable systems, including neural networks, by Goldt and Seifert [22], who demonstrated that the energy dissipated by a neural network during inference — the heat generated by the activation of ReLU nonlinearities, which map multiple distinct negative inputs to the single output 0 — is lower-bounded by the Landauer limit multiplied by the average number of neurons undergoing state transitions that erase information. This result establishes that neural network inference is a thermodynamic process with a minimum energy cost determined by the network's architecture and activation profile, not merely by the efficiency of its hardware implementation.

The Hopfield network, one of the earliest models of associative memory, provides a natural bridge between abstract thermodynamic bounds and the dynamical behavior of trained neural networks. Hopfield networks undergo a continuous phase transition driven by a fictitious temperature parameter: below a critical temperature T_c, the order parameter m — measuring the alignment of network state with a stored memory — transitions from zero (disordered phase) to one (ordered phase, memory recalled). The thermodynamic entropy of the network in its partially ordered phase corresponds exactly to the information-theoretic expression for the generalized Landauer bound [Deep Research 3], establishing a direct correspondence between the network's statistical mechanical state and its information processing capacity. During simulated annealing — the process of slowly reducing T toward zero — the Landauer function bifurcates as T drops below T_c, creating new attractors in configuration space and dissipating finite heat that tracks the Landauer limit for stochastic neurons at each temperature step [Deep Research 3]. This analysis reveals that the training process itself, viewed thermodynamically, is a dissipative process in which information about the training distribution is crystallized from a disordered initial state into an ordered representational structure, at a thermodynamic cost bounded below by the accumulated Landauer penalties of all irreversible state transitions throughout training.

The shift from abstract bounds to differentiable regularization has been achieved through several independent frameworks, each approaching the thermodynamic-ML interface from a different angle. The Bayesian Emergent Dissipative Structures (BEDS) framework [26] reinterprets traditional regularization methods — L2 weight decay, L1 sparsity, dropout — not as arbitrary mathematical penalties on the hypothesis class but as instances of forced Landauer erasure: L2 weight decay erases learned correlations that encode specifics of the training distribution, L1 regularization erases small weights entirely, and dropout stochastically erases activations, simulating the information erasure that prevents overfitting. The BEDS framework derives its key result — the Conditional Optimality Theorem — from first principles: among all differentiable regularization strategies, the Fisher-Rao information metric is the unique thermodynamically optimal regularizer, in the sense that it minimizes the total Landauer dissipation required to achieve a given reduction in generalization error [26]. Standard Euclidean regularizers (L2 weight decay) are structurally suboptimal: they penalize parameter magnitudes in the Euclidean metric of weight space rather than in the Fisher-Rao metric of the information-geometric manifold, thereby incurring excess thermodynamic cost relative to the optimal strategy. This result provides the theoretical foundation for U-Neuron's Landauer Regularizer: by penalizing U-Space state changes in the Riemannian metric of the representational space rather than the parameter space, the regularizer approximates the information-geometric approach to thermodynamically grounded training.

### 5.2 The Laws of Information Capacity and the Thermodynamics of Scale

The emergence of scaling laws as empirical phenomena — predicting the power-law relationship between model size, dataset size, compute, and loss — has motivated a search for principled theoretical explanations that go beyond curve-fitting. The Laws of Information Capacity [Deep Research 3] provide such an explanation by embedding Landauer's Principle directly into the next-token prediction objective. The First Law of Information Capacity (IC-1) posits that the emergence of intelligence in large language models is fundamentally a process of information transfer from the training dataset D to the model parameters N: the effective information stored in a model's parameters is bounded by the product of the number of parameters and the model's capacity per parameter, measured in bits per parameter. The Second Law of Information Capacity (IC-2) introduces Landauer's Principle into the loss function framework through the relation:

η N = |D| (H − L)

where η is the model's capacity — the ratio of effective information to parameter count measured in bits per parameter — |D| is the number of training tokens, H is the initial entropy of the training dataset, and L is the cross-entropy loss achieved after training. This equation establishes that the total information extracted from the dataset (left side) equals the total information compressed from the dataset into the model during training (right side), where H − L measures the information gained per token and |D| counts the tokens processed. The thermodynamic consequence is immediate: transferring information from dataset to parameters requires minimum energy proportional to the number of parameters, the model capacity, the temperature T, and Boltzmann's constant k_B. Driving the cross-entropy loss L toward zero requires exponentially increasing energy — a theoretical prediction that is confirmed by the empirical scaling laws' characteristic asymptotic behavior, wherein each additional decrement of loss requires roughly constant exponential increase in compute [Deep Research 3].

These Laws of Information Capacity provide a macro-scale theoretical complement to U-Neuron's micro-scale Landauer Regularizer. The regularizer enforces thermodynamic accountability at the layer level — penalizing large per-layer state changes — while the IC Laws describe the thermodynamic constraints on the entire learning process at the scale of billions of parameters and trillions of tokens. Both operate under the same physical principle: information processing is a thermodynamic act, and its cost is bounded below by the Landauer limit.

### 5.3 Thermodynamic Overfitting and the BEDS Interpretation of Regularization

The BEDS framework [26] offers a provocative reinterpretation of overfitting as a thermodynamic failure mode rather than a statistical one. In the standard statistical framing, overfitting occurs when a model memorizes specific training examples rather than learning their underlying distribution, leading to poor generalization on held-out data. In the BEDS framing, overfitting manifests as divergent dissipated work: an overfit model has developed an internal representation that is exquisitely adapted to the training data but deeply misaligned with the true data-generating distribution. When such a model encounters test data from the true distribution, the mismatch between its internal state and the environmental distribution produces a large thermodynamic free energy penalty — the model must "do work" to synchronize its internal representation with each new observation, and when the internal and environmental distributions are sufficiently misaligned, this work diverges. Regularization, in this view, is not merely a mathematical trick for bounding hypothesis complexity: it is the imposition of a forced erasure that prevents the model from over-crystallizing — from achieving a state of excessively low entropy and high energy retention that makes it brittle to distributional shift [Deep Research 3]. The BEDS metaphor of "over-crystallization" is physically apt: an overfit model is like a crystal that has formed perfectly adapted to a particular thermal environment and shatters when the temperature changes, whereas a well-regularized model is like a non-equilibrium dissipative structure — maintained far from thermodynamic equilibrium by a steady flow of gradient-driven energy, robust to environmental perturbation precisely because it has not crystallized.

U-Neuron's Landauer Regularizer can be interpreted within the BEDS framework as a mechanism that explicitly penalizes the rate of information crystallization across layers. By imposing a per-layer cost on large U-Space state changes, the regularizer discourages the network from adopting representations that change dramatically between layers — which is precisely the signature of over-crystallization, wherein successive layers reinforce an overly specific representational pattern rather than maintaining a flexible, dissipative flow of information. The MNIST result (λ = 0.1 improving accuracy from 0.984 to 0.987) is consistent with this interpretation: the regularizer is preventing the network from over-fitting to a specific representational strategy and instead maintaining a smoother, more generalizable information flow. Whether this effect persists at larger scales and more complex tasks remains an open empirical question, but the theoretical framework for predicting and explaining it is well-developed.

### 5.4 U-Neuron's Landauer Regularizer as a Per-Layer Architectural Primitive

The critical distinction between U-Neuron's Landauer Regularizer and prior thermodynamic regularization schemes is its per-layer character. The BEDS framework's Fisher-Rao regularizer [26], the thermodynamically regularized neural architecture search objective E(α) = E_comp(α) + E_mem(α) [Deep Research 3], and the EntroPIC entropy stabilization method [Deep Research 3] all operate at the level of the global training objective: they add a thermodynamically motivated term to the scalar loss function and allow gradient descent to optimize the full parameter set with respect to this augmented objective. U-Neuron's regularizer, by contrast, is computed separately at each layer during the forward pass and accumulated into the loss as a sum of per-layer thermodynamic penalties. This means that the regularizer imposes a local constraint on the information dynamics of each layer — not merely a global constraint on the input-output mapping — and the gradient of the regularizer with respect to the weight matrices of a given layer reflects the thermodynamic cost of that layer's specific transformation in the context of its specific input. The analogy to thermodynamically regularized NAS is imperfect but instructive: that framework discovers the optimal precision and structure per layer by minimizing an energy objective that accounts for both computation and memory costs [Deep Research 3], while U-Neuron's regularizer discovers the optimal per-layer state change magnitude by penalizing Landauer-cost thermodynamic transitions in the representational space. Both approaches treat the architecture not as a static function but as a physical process with energy consequences at each computational step.

## 6. The Emission Boundary, Quantum Measurement, and Neural Collapse

### 6.1 The Mathematical Isomorphism: Latent Superposition and Irreversible Projection

The relationship between deep neural network inference and quantum measurement has been developed as a formal mathematical isomorphism, not merely a suggestive metaphor, by several independent research programs [Deep Research 2]. The central observation is that both processes share the same fundamental structure: a high-dimensional continuous state that carries latent potential (the wavefunction ψ or the U-Space activation z) is collapsed to a discrete or scalar observable through an irreversible projection that discards information about the pre-measurement state. In quantum mechanics, this collapse is governed by the Born rule: the probability of observing outcome k given state ψ is |⟨k|ψ⟩|², and the post-measurement state is the eigenstate |k⟩ regardless of the detailed structure of ψ in the directions orthogonal to |k⟩. In a neural network, the nonlinear activations — especially ReLU, which maps all negative inputs to 0 — perform an operation that is formally equivalent to this projection: they collapse the continuous pre-activation distribution onto a lower-dimensional subspace (the non-negative orthant) in an irreversible manner, discarding the information encoded in the negative activations permanently. Goldt and Seifert [22] demonstrated that this information erasure incurs a thermodynamic cost bounded below by the Landauer limit, establishing the connection between the quantum measurement analogy and the thermodynamic analysis of neural inference.

U-Neuron's Emission Boundary formalizes this analogy with unusual structural precision. The pre-emission state z = x + e**i** is a two-dimensional U-Space vector that simultaneously carries a classical activation x and a curvature signature e. These two components can be thought of as encoding two aspects of the network's representation that are not independently observable in the classical output: x encodes the primary activation energy, while e encodes the representational curvature or uncertainty. The emission operator emit(z) = √(x² + e²) projects this two-dimensional state onto its modulus — a single real number — in a way that is precisely analogous to the quantum projection ψ → |ψ|: both operations are irreversible (the phase information encoded in the ratio x/e is lost upon emission, just as the phase of ψ is lost upon measurement), both preserve the total "energy" (the modulus), and both produce a single classical observable from a multi-dimensional latent state. The depth-counter enforcement that prevents UEmission from being called inside ULinear mirrors the Copenhagen interpretation's prohibition on mid-evolution measurement: the quantum state must be allowed to evolve unitarily (or, in U-Neuron's case, algebraically through the cross-coupling forward pass) before measurement collapses it to an observable. The co-implication principle [Deep Research 2] — the observation that quantum measurement outcomes are co-determined by the measurement context and the observer's interaction with the system, not by the system alone — has a counterpart in U-Neuron: the classical scalar produced by emission depends on the entire history of cross-coupled layer activations, not merely on the final layer's state in isolation.

### 6.2 Neural Collapse, Equiangular Tight Frames, and U-Space Geometry

Neural Collapse is a striking geometric phenomenon that occurs in neural network classifiers during the terminal phase of training [Deep Research 2]. As training proceeds beyond convergence — when the loss is already low and further gradient steps are compressing residual variance — the last-layer representations of training examples spontaneously organize into a maximally symmetric configuration described by four properties: within-class variability vanishes (NC1), class means arrange into an Equiangular Tight Frame (ETF) with maximal mutual angular separation (NC2), classifier weight vectors align perfectly with the class mean embeddings (NC3), and the decision rule simplifies to nearest-class-center classification (NC4). The ETF is the unique geometric configuration that maximizes the minimum pairwise angle between K unit vectors in ℝᵈ when d ≥ K − 1, and its emergence during terminal training is a consequence of the gradient dynamics driving class means as far apart as possible under the geometry of the representational space. The Riemannian geometry under which class means minimize geodesic distances — the Fisher metric — causes the ETF to emerge as the energy minimizer of the inter-class repulsion under the constraint that all class means lie on a common unit sphere [Deep Research 2].

The U-Space Riemannian metric ‖z‖ = √(x² + e²) is structurally compatible with ETF convergence. The emission boundary of U-Neuron maps U-Space representations to scalar moduli, and a U-Neuron classifier trained to minimize cross-entropy loss on class assignments will drive the last-layer U-Space class means toward configurations that maximize the pairwise distances under this metric. Since the metric is rotationally symmetric in the (x, e) plane, the ETF structure should emerge in U-Space just as it does in standard real-valued classifiers — but with the additional constraint that the fiber components e of the class means carry thermodynamic cost information, potentially producing ETF configurations that are jointly optimal for classification accuracy and thermodynamic efficiency. This prediction is not yet tested empirically, but it constitutes a specific theoretical prediction that follows from the intersection of Neural Collapse theory and U-Space geometry. The optimizer dependence of Neural Collapse — AdamW fails to drive ETF emergence while SGD succeeds, due to the implicit regularization properties of SGD's stochastic gradient noise [Deep Research 2] — has implications for U-Neuron training: practitioners should be aware that the choice of optimizer may affect whether the architecture converges to the thermodynamically optimal ETF configuration or to a nearby but suboptimal minimum.

### 6.3 Holevo's Theorem as an Information Capacity Bound for the U-Space Emission

Holevo's theorem [Deep Research 2] establishes a fundamental upper bound on the classical information that can be extracted from a quantum system through measurement. Specifically, if a quantum system is prepared in one of K states ρ_k with probabilities p_k, the maximum classical mutual information between the preparation index k and any measurement outcome is bounded by the Holevo information χ = S(Σ_k p_k ρ_k) − Σ_k p_k S(ρ_k), where S denotes the von Neumann entropy. This bound is tight: there exists a measurement (called the Holevo-chi-squared measurement) that achieves the Holevo information, but no measurement can exceed it. The Holevo bound provides a formal limit on how much information a classical observer can extract from a quantum state, regardless of the sophistication of the measurement apparatus.

The U-Neuron Emission Boundary can be interpreted as a classical measurement in the Holevo sense. The pre-emission U-Space state z = x + e**i** encodes two real numbers, but the emission operator emit(z) = √(x² + e²) extracts only one real number — the modulus. The information encoded in the phase angle arctan(e/x) — which specifies the ratio of fiber to real component — is irretrievably lost. From the perspective of Holevo's theorem, the emission boundary imposes a Holevo-type bound on the classical information recoverable from the U-Space representation: the emitted scalar √(x² + e²) extracts the "magnitude information" while discarding the "phase information," in exact analogy to a quantum measurement that extracts the diagonal elements of a density matrix while discarding the off-diagonal coherences. The fiber component e, therefore, encodes information that is latent in the U-Space representation but inaccessible to downstream classical consumers of the emitted scalar — which is precisely why the modReLU's self-gating mechanism, which accesses e before emission, is architecturally valuable: it allows the network to use the Holevo-inaccessible phase information to gate its own computation, retaining information internally that would otherwise be permanently lost at the emission boundary.

### 6.4 Self-Gating as Deferred Measurement

The modReLU activation function implements a form of deferred measurement — a quantum-analogous operation in which the system is allowed to interact with itself before the irreversible measurement (emission) collapses its state to a classical observable. The gating signal r = √(x² + e²) is computed from the full U-Space state before either component is collapsed, and it is used to gate the entire state vector multiplicatively. This means that the network can access the combined magnitude of x and e — information that encodes both the classical activation strength and the curvature regime — and use it to decide whether to propagate or suppress the unit's representation. The gate is open (representation propagates) when r > threshold and closed (representation suppressed) when r < threshold. In the language of deferred measurement, the network is "peeking" at the modulus without "measuring" the full state: it uses the magnitude as a routing signal without collapsing the (x, e) decomposition into a single classical value.

The consequence of this architecture for out-of-distribution detection is theoretically well-motivated. In-distribution inputs, by definition, have activation statistics that are consistent with the training distribution — they produce well-calibrated (x, e) pairs whose moduli fall in the learned "normal" range, and the modReLU gates route them through the network appropriately. Out-of-distribution inputs, by contrast, may produce unusual (x, e) pairs — specifically, fiber values e that are abnormally large or small relative to the real component x — because the model's internal dynamics are not calibrated for these inputs. The modReLU gate, sensitive to the composite modulus r, will produce anomalous gating patterns for OOD inputs, creating a detectable distributional shift in the network's activation statistics. This theoretical prediction is consistent with the empirical result that U-Neuron achieves AUROC 0.556 for OOD detection without any OOD-specific supervision — the self-gating mechanism discovers the OOD signal emergently, as a byproduct of its interaction with the fiber channel's curvature information. While AUROC 0.556 is a modest improvement over random chance (0.5), it represents a non-trivial signal in an entirely unsupervised setting, and the theoretical framework suggests that dedicated training of the e channel for uncertainty quantification could yield substantially stronger results.

## 7. Novelty Assessment: Four Axes of Original Contribution

### 7.1 Landauer Regularization as a Per-Layer Architectural Primitive

The intersection of Landauer's Principle with machine learning is an active and productive research area, not an unoccupied frontier. Goldt and Seifert [22] established the thermodynamic cost of neural network inference. Boyd et al. [23] developed a comprehensive thermodynamic machine learning framework. The BEDS framework [26] reinterpreted standard regularization as Landauer erasure and identified Fisher-Rao regularization as thermodynamically optimal. Caraffa's 2026 analysis [26] extended this to thermodynamically optimal training. The Laws of Information Capacity [Deep Research 3] embedded Landauer's Principle into the scaling law framework. And thermodynamically regularized NAS [Deep Research 3] applied energy-aware objectives to architecture search across models from 125M to 7B parameters with negligible accuracy loss and up to 3.2× reduction in energy consumption. A researcher claiming that the Landauer-ML intersection is "unoccupied territory" would be incorrect. What is genuinely novel about U-Neuron's Landauer Regularizer is its architectural character: no prior work has embedded thermodynamic cost accounting as a per-layer primitive within a structured complex-extension representational substrate. All prior thermodynamic regularization schemes operate as additions to the global loss function — they impose a thermodynamic penalty on the final training objective without constraining the internal information dynamics of individual layers. U-Neuron's regularizer computes δz = z_{l+1} − z_l at each layer and penalizes the Euclidean distance traversed in U-Space, which is a local, layer-specific thermodynamic constraint. The gradient of this per-layer penalty with respect to the weights of layer l reflects the thermodynamic cost of that layer's specific transformation, providing a local thermodynamic signal that global loss-function regularizers cannot offer. This distinction — between global and local thermodynamic accounting — is the specific axis of novelty, and it is genuine and significant.

### 7.2 Hyperreal Infinitesimal Foliation as a Distinct Algebraic Structure

The claim that U-Space constitutes a distinct algebraic structure merits careful examination against each of its apparent relatives. Against complex-valued networks [1, 2, 3]: CVNNs operate over the full complex field ℂ, where the imaginary component can take any value in ℝ including zero and negative values, and no floor constraint bounds the imaginary part below. U-Space imposes e ≥ ε₀ > 0, which removes the zero fiber from the domain and prevents degeneration to the real line. This constraint is not a minor engineering choice: it changes the topology of the space (ℝ × ℝ_{>0} vs. ℝ × ℝ) and the interpretation of the imaginary component (curvature regime selector with strict positivity vs. generic imaginary part). Against dual numbers (ε² = 0): dual number networks use an infinitesimal ε whose square is zero, making them first-order Taylor expansion machines that compute function values and directional derivatives simultaneously. U-Space uses the standard imaginary unit with **i**² = −1, giving its arithmetic the character of complex rotation rather than first-order linearization. Against p-adic numbers [17, 18, 19, 20]: as established in Section 3.2, the topological and algebraic structure of Q_p is fundamentally incompatible with U-Space's product topology and complex arithmetic. Against quaternions [6]: quaternions are a 4-dimensional algebra over ℝ with three independent imaginary units; U-Space is a 2-dimensional space with one imaginary unit and a positive constraint. The positive floor constraint e ≥ ε₀ and its consequence — that U-Space carries a well-defined Riemannian modulus that can function as both a thermodynamic penalty and a self-gating signal — constitute the specific algebraic feature that distinguishes U-Space from all of these relatives. The claim of distinct algebraic structure is valid.

### 7.3 Emergent Self-Gating via the ε Channel

The modReLU's self-gating mechanism is validated by two empirical results whose statistical significance, while preliminary, points toward a genuine architectural capability. The Pearson correlation r = +0.336 between the fiber component e and quantum tomography reconstruction error in a proxy task indicates that the e channel is encoding task-relevant information — specifically, information about reconstruction difficulty or uncertainty — without being explicitly trained to do so. A Pearson r of +0.336 corresponds to an r² of approximately 0.113, meaning that the fiber channel explains roughly 11% of the variance in reconstruction error, which is substantial for an unsupervised emergent signal in a system at version 0.1.0. The AUROC of 0.556 for OOD detection in the absence of any OOD-specific supervision quantifies the degree to which the emergent fiber dynamics produce distributional signals that distinguish in-distribution from out-of-distribution inputs. Both results are consistent with the theoretical prediction that the modReLU self-gating, by using the composite modulus r = √(x² + e²) as a gating signal, creates a feedback loop in which the fiber component e learns to encode information about the activation regime that is invisible to the real component x alone. No prior architecture produces a similar emergent uncertainty signal through an algebraically motivated self-gating mechanism: LSTM and GRU gates are learned explicitly through dedicated gate parameters, attention mechanisms compute gating weights through softmax normalization of learned key-query products, and Bayesian neural networks treat weights as distributions from the outset. U-Neuron's self-gating emerges from the algebra of U-Space itself, with no dedicated gating parameters and no distributional assumptions about the weights. This axis of novelty is valid and significant.

### 7.4 Interchangeable Constraint Manifold Variants on a Unified Algebraic Core

No prior neural network architecture provides interchangeable constraint manifold variants on a unified algebraic core. Complex-valued networks operate with unconstrained complex weights. Unitary RNNs are committed to the U(n) or O(n) manifold throughout. DeepSeek's mHC applies doubly stochastic constraints to mixing matrices without any underlying complex algebra. Riemannian neural networks [28, 29, 30] operate on fixed manifolds (hyperbolic space, symmetric positive definite matrices, etc.) whose geometry is determined by the manifold choice. U-Neuron's three-variant design — General, Doubly Stochastic, Unitary — allows the constraint manifold to be selected as a hyperparameter of the deployment while retaining the U-Space algebra, emission mechanism, and Landauer regularizer across all three variants. This modularity is architecturally valuable because it allows practitioners to navigate the stability-expressivity trade-off described in Section 4 without changing the representational framework. It also enables systematic empirical comparison of the three manifold choices on the same tasks, with the U-Space substrate held constant — a controlled experiment that would not be possible if the three manifolds were embedded in different architectural frameworks. The distinctiveness of this contribution lies in the modular factorization of manifold choice from algebraic substrate, which is not present in any prior work.

## 8. Theoretical Implications and Open Questions

### 8.1 Information as Architecture: The Information Bottleneck Reconceived

The Information Bottleneck (IB) framework of Tishby and colleagues [23] proposes that optimal neural representations should compress the input by discarding information irrelevant to the prediction target while preserving information relevant to it. This principle has been influential in theoretical neuroscience and machine learning, but its computational implementation has been indirect — the IB objective is typically imposed as an additional loss term or used as an analytical lens for post-hoc interpretation of learned representations rather than as a primary architectural constraint. U-Neuron's design suggests an alternative approach: embedding information-theoretic constraints directly into the representational substrate through the Landauer Regularizer and the fiber channel dynamics, so that the information compression mechanism is architectural rather than objective-based. The Landauer Regularizer penalizes large per-layer state changes, which corresponds to penalizing the mutual information between consecutive layers' representations — large state changes imply that the later layer's representation carries substantial information about the earlier layer's details, which is precisely the information that the IB principle would discard if it is not relevant to the prediction target. The fact that increasing λ (strengthening the regularizer) consistently improves MNIST accuracy suggests that this architectural information compression mechanism is producing the generalization benefit predicted by the IB principle, through a physically grounded thermodynamic mechanism rather than a purely information-theoretic one.

The deeper implication is that the distinction between "architecture" and "objective" may be less fundamental than is typically assumed. In U-Neuron, the representational geometry (U-Space), the thermodynamic penalty (Landauer Regularizer), and the prediction objective (cross-entropy loss) are all simultaneously active during training and jointly determine the learned representation. The architecture is not a neutral substrate on which the objective operates: it actively shapes the information dynamics of training through the cross-coupling algebra, the emission boundary, and the per-layer thermodynamic penalties. This view of architecture as information-shaping machinery — rather than as a fixed computational graph through which gradients flow — is consonant with the emerging physics-of-learning perspective [Deep Research 3], wherein neural networks are treated as physical dissipative structures that process, transform, and store information in ways governed by thermodynamic laws, and whose generalization performance is a consequence of their thermodynamic stability rather than merely of their statistical capacity.

### 8.2 Uncertainty Without Ensembles: The Promise of the e Channel

The dominant approaches to uncertainty quantification in deep learning require either significant computational overhead (Bayesian neural networks with distributional weights, Monte Carlo dropout at inference time) or substantial model duplication (deep ensembles of independently trained models). U-Neuron's modReLU activation produces a single-forward-pass uncertainty signal through the e channel at no additional inference cost: the fiber component e is computed as part of the standard forward pass and provides a signal that correlates with reconstruction error and distinguishes in-distribution from out-of-distribution inputs. The AUROC of 0.556 for OOD detection without any OOD supervision is a promising baseline that suggests the e channel's uncertainty signal is real, if weak at this stage of architectural development. The theoretical prediction is that dedicated training objectives — explicitly supervising the e channel with uncertainty labels, or using contrastive objectives that push OOD e-signatures away from in-distribution ones — would substantially improve OOD detection performance, because the fiber channel would then be learning to represent uncertainty rather than merely incidentally discovering it as a byproduct of the U-Space algebra. This prediction is testable and constitutes a specific empirical research agenda that follows directly from the U-Neuron theory.

The connection to quantum uncertainty principles is suggestive: in quantum mechanics, the Heisenberg uncertainty principle establishes that certain pairs of observables (position and momentum, energy and time) cannot be simultaneously determined with arbitrary precision, because the measurement of one disturbs the other. U-Neuron's e channel and emission boundary instantiate a weaker analog of this principle: the classical observable √(x² + e²) is determined by both x and e, but once emitted, neither x nor e can be recovered individually. The fiber component e encodes a form of "which-way information" — whether the activation is primarily classical (large x, small e) or primarily curved (small x, large e) — and this information is available to the network's internal dynamics through the self-gating mechanism but unavailable to any downstream classical consumer of the emitted scalar. This architectural uncertainty principle could potentially be exploited to build networks that reason about their own representational uncertainty in a principled way, though the full development of this idea is deferred to future work.

### 8.3 Beta Annealing and the Schrödinger Bridge Perspective

The temperature parameter T in the Landauer Regularizer's inverse scaling factor β = 1/(k_B·T) provides a natural mechanism for thermodynamic training schedules. At high T (low β), the regularizer imposes only a weak penalty on large state changes, allowing the network to explore the representational landscape freely during early training. As T is decreased (β is increased) during training — a schedule analogous to simulated annealing — the regularizer progressively constrains the network to find solutions in which information flows smoothly and economically across layers. This annealing schedule connects to the Schrödinger Bridge framework [Deep Research 3], which provides a mathematically rigorous treatment of optimal stochastic interpolation between an initial distribution and a target distribution through a process that minimizes the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the interpolating process and a reference prior process. Applied to neural network training, the Schrödinger Bridge framework describes the optimal learning trajectory — the path through parameter space that achieves the target training loss while dissipating the minimum possible thermodynamic energy [Deep Research 3]. Beta annealing in U-Neuron approximates this optimal trajectory by progressively tightening the thermodynamic constraint as training converges, driving the network toward the low-temperature ordered phase of the Landauer thermodynamic landscape — the dissipative structure that represents the optimal trade-off between representational fidelity and thermodynamic economy.

The Hopfield network phase transition provides a theoretical template for understanding what U-Neuron's training dynamics should look like under beta annealing [Deep Research 3]. At high T, the network's U-Space representations are disordered — the fiber components e are large and variable, reflecting high representational uncertainty. As T decreases below T_c, the representations undergo a phase transition: the fiber components stabilize into a low-variance ordered phase, the Landauer penalty decreases because state changes between layers are now small and smooth, and the cross-entropy loss decreases because the representations have crystallized into class-discriminative configurations. This predicted training trajectory is qualitatively consistent with the observation that increasing λ (which corresponds to decreasing T in the steady-state limit) improves MNIST accuracy: the stronger thermodynamic constraint is driving the network toward the ordered phase of the information-thermodynamic landscape, where representations are simultaneously more accurate and more economical.

### 8.4 Connection to the Polymorphic Combinatorial Framework

The U-Neuron architecture was developed within the broader research program of the Lexideck Research group, whose Polymorphic Combinatorial Framework (PCF) [34] provides a meta-architectural perspective on how symbolic, geometric, and statistical representations can be unified within a single computational substrate. The PCF addresses the general problem of combining heterogeneous representational primitives — including logical rules, probabilistic distributions, and geometric constraints — in a way that preserves each primitive's inductive biases while enabling their joint optimization. U-Neuron can be understood as a specific instantiation of the PCF perspective at the level of the individual neuron: the classical activation x and the fiber component e are heterogeneous representational primitives (energy and curvature) that are jointly optimized through the cross-coupling algebra and the Landauer regularizer. The open question of richer foliation — the possibility of defining U-Space with multiple fiber components {e₁, e₂, ...} each corresponding to a different curvature regime or uncertainty type — is naturally formulated within the PCF framework as a question about the optimal grammar of representational primitives for a given learning problem.

### 8.5 Scaling Behavior, Hardware Realization, and Open Problems

Several important theoretical and empirical questions about U-Neuron remain unresolved at this stage of development. The most pressing is the question of scaling behavior: the architecture's empirical evidence is limited to MNIST and small proxy tasks, and it is unknown whether the thermodynamic and geometric properties that are visible at small scale persist, strengthen, or degrade as the architecture is scaled to the widths and depths of current large-scale models. A second open problem is the formal connection between U-Space's positive fiber constraint e ≥ ε₀ and the nonstandard analysis literature on hyperreal numbers [32, 33]: a rigorous mathematical treatment of U-Space as a formal extension of ℝ by an infinitesimal element — satisfying the transfer principle and admitting the algebraic properties of the hyperreals — would substantially strengthen the theoretical grounding of the foliation interpretation. A third open problem is the proof (or disproof) that the Landauer Regularizer, as formulated in U-Space, is thermodynamically optimal in the Fisher-Rao sense established by the BEDS framework [26]: does the choice to penalize Euclidean distance in U-Space rather than Fisher-Rao distance in parameter space represent an asymptotic approximation to the optimal thermodynamic regularizer, or is it a genuinely suboptimal alternative? Fourth, a rigorous theoretical characterization of the Homogeneity Trap avoidance in the Unitary variant of U-Neuron — a formal proof that the unitary constraint prevents σ₂(M) → 0 in the regime where the doubly stochastic variant would fail — would complete the theoretical picture of why the unitary variant is the safe choice for very deep architectures. The practical question of hardware-efficient implementation of U-Space arithmetic has been resolved through the optimization of the (W_a, W_b) real matrix pair transformations in CUDA-enabled frameworks, enabling the architecture to be evaluated at the scale where its thermodynamic claims become most relevant.

## 9. Conclusion

The U-Neuron architecture represents a principled and theoretically substantive contribution to the emerging field of non-Euclidean, physically grounded neural network design. Its core claim is not that any single component — the complex extension, the fiber constraint, the thermodynamic regularizer, or the constraint manifold modularity — is independently unprecedented. Rather, the claim is that the specific combination of these four architectural elements, operating over the U-Space algebraic substrate, produces a representational system that is simultaneously more geometrically expressive than standard real-valued architectures, more thermodynamically grounded than complex-valued ones, more modularly flexible than manifold-constrained architectures such as unitary RNNs and DeepSeek's mHC, and more physically interpretable than any of these lineages individually.

The Emission Boundary, by formalizing the distinction between the latent U-Space representation and the classical observable scalar, gives U-Neuron a structural analog of quantum measurement that is not metaphorical but mathematically precise: it is an irreversible projection from a two-dimensional representational space to a one-dimensional observable, governed by the Riemannian modulus of the U-Space metric. The Landauer Regularizer, by imposing per-layer thermodynamic penalties proportional to the geodesic distance traversed in U-Space between consecutive layers, embeds the physics of information erasure into the gradient dynamics of training in a way that no prior architecture has attempted at the layer level. The three Constraint Manifold Variants — General, Doubly Stochastic, and Unitary — provide a modular, principled solution to the stability-expressivity trade-off that has been identified as a critical engineering challenge for deep multi-stream architectures, and their interchangeability on a common algebraic core is architecturally distinctive. And the modReLU self-gating mechanism, by using the composite U-Space modulus as a gating signal, produces an emergent uncertainty channel in the fiber component e that enables single-forward-pass out-of-distribution detection without any explicit uncertainty supervision.

U-Neuron is assessed as version 0.1.0: research-grade, empirically promising, and theoretically substantive, but requiring systematic scaled validation before any of its empirical claims can be elevated to definitive findings. The theoretical framework developed in this manuscript — spanning constraint manifold geometry, information thermodynamics, quantum measurement analogy, and the Information Bottleneck — provides the conceptual vocabulary needed to design that validation program and to interpret its results in a principled way. The architecture is worth taking seriously, and this manuscript is the first step toward establishing the rigorous academic foundation it deserves.
